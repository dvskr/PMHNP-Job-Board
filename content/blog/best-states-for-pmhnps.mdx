Where you choose to practice doesn't just determine your paycheck. It defines the scope of your clinical authority, your take-home pay after taxes, your housing costs, and your overall quality of life. As an illustration, put two PMHNPs on identical $165,000 offers: one in Washington (FPA, no state income tax), one in California (tighter practice rules, state income tax, much higher housing costs). Same gross, very different money left at the end of the month.

This guide ranks states not just by salary, but by the factors that actually determine your financial and professional outcome.

## Understanding Practice Authority: Why It Matters

Your ability to practice independently, prescribe without co-signatures, and open your own practice depends entirely on state law.

### Full Practice Authority (FPA) States

*You practice independently: no physician oversight required.*

These states allow PMHNPs to assess, diagnose, prescribe (including controlled substances), and manage patients independently from day one (or after a brief transition period):

WA, OR, NV, AZ, NM, CO, WY, MT, ID, ND, SD, MN, IA, NE, KS, UT, ME, NH, VT, MA, RI, CT, NY (after 3,600 hours), MD, DE, VA (after 5 years), DC, HI, AK

### Reduced Practice Authority States

*You need a collaboration agreement but can practice with general supervision.*

CA, TX, IL, IN, WI, NJ, PA (varies by specialty)

### Restricted Practice Authority States

*You need direct physician oversight and/or co-signing of prescriptions.*

FL, GA, NC, SC, TN, AL, MS, MO, OK, MI, OH

**Why FPA matters financially:** In FPA states, you can open a private practice without paying a collaborating physician a monthly supervision fee, which is a real and recurring cost in restricted states. That directly increases your income. FPA states also have faster credentialing, fewer administrative hurdles, and more practice options.

For your state's specific practice requirements, see our [Full Practice Authority Guide](/resources/fpa-guide) and [50-state licensing guides](/jobs/locations).

### High Tax States (Where Your Salary Is Misleading)

The column below applies each state's top marginal rate to an illustrative $180,000 across the whole amount. Real liability comes out lower, because brackets are graduated, but the gap between states holds.

| State | Top Tax Rate | Illustrative impact on $180K |
|-------|-------------|----------------------|
| California | 13.3% | -$23,940/year |
| New York | 10.9% (+NYC 3.9%) | -$19,620-$26,640/year |
| New Jersey | 10.75% | -$19,350/year |
| Oregon | 9.9% | -$17,820/year |
| Minnesota | 9.85% | -$17,730/year |

**The takeaway:** State tax alone can swallow a five-figure slice of a high salary. An offer in a no-tax state needs a meaningfully smaller gross to land in the same place as a California or New York offer, before cost of living differences even enter the picture.

## Where Should You Move? A Decision Framework

### For Maximum Take-Home Pay:
→ **Washington State** or **Nevada** (FPA + no income tax + strong salaries)

### For Private Practice Independence:
→ **Arizona** or **Colorado** (FPA + growing markets + low barriers to entry)

### For Maximum Raw Salary:
→ **California** or **New York** (but prepare for high taxes and COL)

### For Low Cost of Living + High Relative Value:
→ **Iowa, Nebraska, or Kansas** (FPA + very low COL + competitive salaries relative to expenses)

### For VA/Federal Employment:
→ **Any state**: the VA provides FPA nationwide regardless of state law, with consistent federal pay scales

### For New Grads Seeking Mentorship:
→ **States with large health systems**: Massachusetts, New York, California, and Texas have the most residency and fellowship programs
