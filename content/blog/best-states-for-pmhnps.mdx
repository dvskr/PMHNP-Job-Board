Where you choose to practice doesn't just determine your paycheck. It defines the scope of your clinical authority, your take-home pay after taxes, your housing costs, and your overall quality of life. As an illustration, put two PMHNPs on identical $165,000 offers: one in Washington (FPA, no state income tax), one in California (tighter practice rules, state income tax, much higher housing costs). Same gross, very different money left at the end of the month.

This guide ranks states not just by salary, but by the factors that actually determine your financial and professional outcome.

## Understanding Practice Authority: Why It Matters

Your ability to practice independently, prescribe without co-signatures, and open your own practice depends entirely on state law.

### Full Practice Authority (FPA) States

*You practice independently: no physician oversight required.*

These states allow PMHNPs to assess, diagnose, prescribe (including controlled substances), and manage patients independently from day one (or after a brief transition period):

WA, OR, NV, AZ, NM, CO, WY, MT, ID, ND, SD, MN, IA, NE, KS, UT, ME, NH, VT, MA, RI, CT, NY (after 3,600 hours), MD, DE, DC, HI, AK

### Reduced Practice Authority States

*You need a collaboration agreement but can practice with general supervision.*

AL, AR, IL, IN, KY, LA, MS, NJ, OH, PA, WV, WI

### Restricted Practice Authority States

*You need physician supervision for prescribing and some clinical decisions.*

CA, TX, FL, GA, NC, SC, TN, MO, OK, MI, VA

**Why FPA matters financially:** In FPA states, you can open a private practice without paying a collaborating physician a monthly supervision fee, which is a real and recurring cost in restricted states. That directly increases your income. FPA states also have faster credentialing, fewer administrative hurdles, and more practice options. If you plan to work for a hospital or large health system instead, the rules weigh less: in reduced and restricted states, these employers often arrange the collaborating or supervising physician for you.

**Paths to independence.** The tiers above describe the default rules, and several reduced and restricted states now let experienced NPs go further:

- **Virginia:** NPs with the equivalent of three years of full-time clinical experience can practice without a practice agreement once that experience is attested ([Code of Virginia § 54.1-2957](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title54.1/chapter29/section54.1-2957/)).
- **California:** the independent 104 NP category is open to NPs with three years in good standing as a 103 NP and a 4,600 hour transition to practice.
- **Wisconsin:** since September 1, 2026, APRNs can practice independently after 3,840 RN hours and 3,840 advanced practice hours working with a physician or dentist.
- **New Jersey:** a law signed March 30, 2026 lets qualifying APNs in primary or behavioral health care prescribe without a joint protocol.
- **Florida:** autonomous practice is allowed only in primary care, and the board's rule defines primary care to include behavioral and mental health conditions.
- **Oklahoma:** independent prescriptive authority, excluding Schedule II, after 6,240 supervised clinical practice hours; the state is still not full practice.

Check any state's current classification on our [practice authority map](/tools/practice-authority-map). For your state's specific practice requirements, see our [Full Practice Authority Guide](/resources/fpa-guide) and [50-state licensing guides](/resources).

## Where Should You Move? A Decision Framework

### For Maximum Take-Home Pay:
→ **Washington State** or **Nevada** (FPA + no income tax + strong salaries)

### For Private Practice Independence:
→ **Arizona** or **Colorado** (FPA + growing markets + low barriers to entry)

### For Maximum Raw Salary:
→ **California** or **New York** (but prepare for high taxes and COL)

### For Low Cost of Living + High Relative Value:
→ **Iowa, Nebraska, or Kansas** (FPA + very low COL + competitive salaries relative to expenses)

### For VA/Federal Employment:
→ **Any state**: VA nurse practitioners work on federal pay scales, and a federal rule ([38 CFR 17.415](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/38/17.415)) lets the VA grant them full practice authority within their VA employment, regardless of state law. One exception matters for PMHNPs: controlled substance prescribing still follows federal law and your state license.

### For New Grads Seeking Mentorship:
→ **States with large health systems and established residency or fellowship programs.** Our [PMHNP residency and fellowship directory](/blog/pmhnp-residency-fellowship-programs-2026-directory-how-to-apply) lists programs by location, including California, Massachusetts, New York, and North Carolina.

## Before You Move: Licenses, Telehealth Prescribing, and Loan Repayment

Four more checks shape where you can practice and what you keep:

- **Licenses:** there is no working multistate license for NPs yet. The APRN Compact has not taken effect, and the Nurse Licensure Compact covers RN and LPN licenses only, so plan on a separate APRN license for each state where your patients are located. See our [multi-state licensure guide](/resources/multi-state-licensure).
- **Telehealth prescribing:** federal rules allow telemedicine prescribing of controlled substances without a prior in-person evaluation through December 31, 2026. State law still applies, and the DEA requires a separate registration for each state where you practice. See our [multi-state DEA guide](/blog/multi-state-dea-guide-telehealth-pmhnp-2026).
- **Loan repayment:** federal programs such as the National Health Service Corps and Nurse Corps repay part of your loans in exchange for a service commitment, so ask whether a job qualifies before you accept.
- **Malpractice coverage:** premiums vary by state, so price a policy before you plan a private practice.
