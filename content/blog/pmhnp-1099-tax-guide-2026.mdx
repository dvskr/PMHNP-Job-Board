If you're a PMHNP working as a 1099 independent contractor, whether through a telehealth platform, private practice, or consulting arrangement, you're essentially running a business. The IRS treats you as self-employed, and the tax implications are significant. Without proper planning, taxes can take well over a third of your income. With strategic planning, you can bring that share down substantially.

This guide covers everything you need to know to keep more of what you earn.

## Understanding 1099 vs W-2 Taxation

| Tax Component | W-2 Employee | 1099 Independent Contractor |
|--------------|-------------|---------------------------|
| **Federal income tax** | Yes | Yes |
| **State income tax** | Yes (if applicable) | Yes (if applicable) |
| **Social Security (6.2%)** | Employer pays half | You pay **both halves** (12.4%) |
| **Medicare (1.45%)** | Employer pays half | You pay **both halves** (2.9%) |
| **Self-employment tax** | N/A | **15.3%** on net earnings |
| **Additional Medicare** | 0.9% on income >$200K | 0.9% on income >$200K |
| **Withholding** | Automatic from paycheck | You must pay quarterly |

Every rate in that table is set by federal statute and administered by the IRS, including the $200,000 filing-status threshold at which the Additional Medicare Tax starts. Confirm the current thresholds at irs.gov.

**The key difference:** As a W-2 employee, your employer pays half of Social Security and Medicare taxes (7.65%). As a 1099 contractor, you pay the full 15.3% yourself. On $200,000 of net income, for example, that's an extra $15,300 in self-employment tax alone.

## Quarterly Estimated Tax Payments

The IRS requires 1099 earners to pay taxes quarterly: you can't wait until April 15th:

| Quarter | Income Period | Payment Due |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Q1 | January - March | **April 15** |
| Q2 | April - May | **June 15** |
| Q3 | June - August | **September 15** |
| Q4 | September - December | **January 15 (next year)** |

### How to Calculate Quarterly Payments

1. **Estimate your annual net income** (gross revenue minus deductible expenses)
2. **Calculate total tax liability** (federal income tax + self-employment tax + state tax)
3. **Divide by 4** for quarterly payments

**Rule of thumb:** Set aside **30-35%** of every 1099 payment you receive into a separate savings account for taxes. Do not touch this money.

**Underpayment penalty:** If you don't pay enough in quarterly estimates, the IRS charges an underpayment penalty at a rate it resets every quarter, so check the current rate at irs.gov. You avoid it entirely by meeting the IRS safe harbor: pay at least 100% of your prior year's tax liability, or 110% if your prior-year income exceeded $150,000.

## Tax-Deductible Business Expenses

Every legitimate business expense reduces your taxable income. Common PMHNP deductions:

### Clinical Expenses
| Expense | Who Sets the Price | Fully Deductible? |
|---------|---------------------|--------------------|
| Malpractice insurance | Your carrier, priced by state and scope of practice | ✅ Yes |
| State APRN licenses (all states) | Each state board of nursing sets its own fee, and multi-state clinicians pay several | ✅ Yes |
| DEA registration(s) | The DEA charges $888 for a three-year practitioner registration; verify the current fee with the DEA | ✅ Yes |
| ANCC certification & renewal | The ANCC publishes the exam and renewal fees, with a discount for members of its partner associations | ✅ Yes |
| Professional memberships (AANP, APNA, APA) | Each association sets its own annual dues | ✅ Yes |
| CME courses and conferences | Providers and conference organizers | ✅ Yes |
| CME-related travel (flights, hotels, meals) | Deductible when the primary purpose of the trip is CME | ✅ Yes |
| EHR subscription (SimplePractice, etc.) | Priced per clinician by the vendor | ✅ Yes |
| Prescribing references (UpToDate, Epocrates) | Annual subscription set by the publisher | ✅ Yes |
| CAQH ProView | Free | N/A |

### Home Office Expenses
| Expense | Deduction Method | Notes |
|---------|-----------------|-------|
| **Simplified method** | The IRS rate per square foot, capped at 300 sq ft | Easy; no receipts needed. Check the IRS rate for the filing year. |
| **Actual expense method** | % of home used for business × (rent/mortgage + utilities + insurance + repairs) | Higher deduction but requires documentation |
| **Internet** | Business % (typically 50-75%) | Must be your primary work connection |
| **Phone** | Business % (typically 50%) | Dedicated business line is 100% deductible |
| **Computer/monitors/webcam** | 100% if used exclusively for business | Section 179 immediate deduction |
| **Office furniture** | 100% if used exclusively for business | Ergonomic chair, standing desk, etc. |

### Other Deductions
- **Health insurance premiums:** 100% deductible as a self-employed individual (above-the-line deduction)
- **Retirement contributions:** SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k) contributions are fully deductible (see below)
- **Business insurance:** general liability, cyber liability
- **Accounting/bookkeeping:** CPA fees, QuickBooks, accounting software
- **Business bank account fees:** monthly fees, transaction fees
- **Marketing/advertising:** Psychology Today profile, website, SEO, business cards
- **Vehicle/mileage:** the IRS standard mileage rate for business travel (SNF visits, driving between facilities, conferences). The IRS republishes the rate each year, so use the current one.

## The S-Corp Strategy: Cutting Self-Employment Tax

The S-Corporation election is the single most impactful tax strategy for high-earning 1099 PMHNPs. Here's how it works:

### How S-Corp Taxation Works

Without S-Corp (sole proprietor): All net income is subject to 15.3% self-employment tax.

With S-Corp: You split your income into two buckets:
1. **Reasonable salary** → Subject to FICA/Medicare (15.3% total, split between you and your S-Corp)
2. **Distributions** → NOT subject to self-employment tax

### S-Corp Savings Example

Illustrative arithmetic, not a promise about your return:

| | No S-Corp (Sole Prop) | With S-Corp |
|---|---------------------|-------------|
| Net business income | $220,000 | $220,000 |
| "Reasonable salary" | N/A | $135,000 |
| Distributions | N/A | $85,000 |
| SE tax base | $220,000 | $135,000 (salary only) |
| Self-employment tax | **$31,028** | **$20,655** |
| **Annual savings** | n/a | **$10,373** |

*The higher your net income sits above a defensible reasonable salary, the larger the distribution bucket and the bigger the saving. Run your own numbers with a CPA rather than assuming this example is yours.*

### S-Corp Requirements and Costs
- **Formation:** File Form 2553 with the IRS (free), plus the LLC or corporation filing fee that your state's secretary of state sets
- **Payroll:** Must run payroll for yourself (W-2 from your own S-Corp), and payroll services such as Gusto or ADP charge an annual fee for it
- **Tax returns:** Separate S-Corp tax return (Form 1120-S) in addition to your personal return, so your CPA's fee rises accordingly
- **Reasonable salary:** the IRS requires your salary to be "reasonable" for your role, meaning roughly what you'd earn as a W-2 PMHNP in your market. [Current advertised pay by state](/salary-guide) is a defensible starting point for that comparison.

### When to Elect S-Corp

| Net Annual Income | S-Corp Worth It? | Reason |
|-------------------|------------------|--------|
| < $80,000 | ❌ No | Savings don't outweigh the costs and complexity |
| $80,000 - $120,000 | 🟡 Maybe | Run the numbers with a CPA |
| > $120,000 | ✅ Yes | The self-employment tax saved usually outweighs the payroll and filing costs |

## Retirement Accounts: Tax-Deferred Wealth Building

As a 1099 earner, you have access to more powerful retirement accounts than most W-2 employees:

| Account | How the Limit Works | Best For |
|---------|----------------------|----------|
| **SEP-IRA** | 25% of net self-employment income, up to the IRS annual cap | Simple setup, high contribution limits |
| **Solo 401(k)** | Employee deferral plus an employer contribution of up to 25% of compensation, subject to the IRS combined cap | Maximum flexibility, Roth option available |
| **Traditional IRA** | The IRS annual IRA limit, plus a catch-up amount once you turn 50 | Supplemental; may not be deductible at high income |
| **Roth IRA** | The same IRS annual limit, via the backdoor route if your income is too high to contribute directly | Tax-free growth |
| **HSA** (if eligible) | The IRS annual HSA limit for individual or family coverage | Triple tax advantage if on HDHP |

The IRS resets every one of these caps each year, so pull the current figures from irs.gov before you fund an account.

**Example impact:** Contributing $50,000 to a Solo 401(k) on $220,000 of net income drops your taxable income to $170,000. At a 24% marginal federal rate, that is $12,000 kept before any state saving. Keep it up and the compounding matters more than the deduction: at a 7% average annual return, $50,000 contributed every year for 20 years grows past $2 million.

## When to Hire a CPA

You need a CPA, not TurboTax, if:
- You earn more than $100K as a 1099 contractor
- You're considering S-Corp election
- You have multi-state licenses and work across state lines
- You're starting or running a private practice
- You want to optimize retirement contributions

**What to look for:** A CPA who specializes in healthcare professionals or small business taxation. Expect a four-figure annual fee for comprehensive tax planning and return preparation, and get the quote in writing before you engage.

## The Bottom Line

1099 PMHNP work can be lucrative, but only if you manage the tax implications proactively. The difference between passive and strategic tax management is real money every single year. Set aside 30-35% for taxes, maximize deductions, consider S-Corp election above $120K income, fund retirement aggressively, and work with a qualified CPA.
