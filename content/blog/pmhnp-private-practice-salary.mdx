Private practice is the highest-earning path for most PMHNPs, but it is not a guaranteed windfall. Your actual income depends on how you structure your practice, where you practice, and how many patients you see. Here is what the published data shows, and where it runs out.

## Average Private Practice PMHNP Salary

According to multiple salary databases (2024-2026 data):

| Source | Average Salary | Range |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ZipRecruiter (2026) | $165,094 | $123,000 - $333,000 |
| Zippia | $155,000 | $102,000 - $211,000 |
| Nightingale College (2025) | $151,588 | Varies by state |
| Research.com (2024) | $154,475 | $140,000 - $200,000+ |

**Key context:** These averages mix part-time solo practitioners with full-time practice owners running multi-provider clinics. The distribution is heavily right-skewed, meaning a small percentage of high earners pulls the average up. All four are commercial salary aggregators built on self-reported and scraped data rather than a government survey. For an independent benchmark, the Bureau of Labor Statistics puts nurse practitioners at **$155,000 to $165,000** ([BLS OEWS 29-1171, Nurse Practitioners, May 2024](https://www.bls.gov/oes/current/oes291171.htm)). That figure covers all NP specialties, not PMHNPs specifically.

## Income by Practice Model

Not all private practices are created equal. The tables below are planning scenarios, not survey results: treat visits per week, rate per visit, and overhead as dials you set for your own market, and the revenue and net lines as the arithmetic that follows. For measured pay on live listings, see the [PMHNP salary guide](/salary-guide).

### 1. Solo Practice: Cash Pay Only

| Metric | Planning range |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Patients/week** | 15-25 |
| **Rate/visit** | $200-$400 (initial), $150-$250 (follow-up) |
| **Gross revenue** | $200,000-$350,000/year |
| **Overhead** | 15-25% (telehealth) to 30-40% (office) |
| **Net income** | $150,000-$280,000 |

Cash-pay practices eliminate insurance billing complexity, but limit your patient pool. Best suited for urban/suburban areas with higher income demographics.

### 2. Solo Practice: Insurance-Based

| Metric | Planning range |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Patients/week** | 25-40 |
| **Reimbursement** | $90-$180/visit (varies wildly by payer) |
| **Gross revenue** | $180,000-$300,000/year |
| **Overhead** | 25-40% (billing, credentialing, EHR) |
| **Net income** | $120,000-$200,000 |

Insurance panels provide steady patient volume, but lower per-visit reimbursement and significant administrative burden. Credentialing takes 3-6 months.

### 3. Telehealth Private Practice

| Metric | Planning range |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Patients/week** | 20-35 |
| **Rate/visit** | $175-$350 (cash) or $100-$180 (insurance) |
| **Gross revenue** | $180,000-$400,000/year |
| **Overhead** | 10-20% (no office lease!) |
| **Net income** | $160,000-$350,000 |

Telehealth dramatically reduces overhead (no rent, no commute, lower malpractice premiums). Many of the highest-earning PMHNPs run virtual practices. Platforms like Headway, Alma, and SimplePractice make it easier to launch.

### 4. Group Practice Owner (2+ Providers)

| Metric | Planning range |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Practice revenue** | $500,000-$2M+/year |
| **Owner's clinical income** | $150,000-$250,000 |
| **Management/profit income** | $50,000-$200,000+ |
| **Total owner income** | $200,000-$450,000+ |

Scaling beyond solo practice is where the biggest incomes come from. You hire other PMHNPs or therapists, take a percentage of their billings, and add management income on top of your own clinical revenue.

## Worked Income Scenarios

The four scenarios below are arithmetic, not case studies. Each one states its inputs so you can swap in your own.

> **Scenario 1: solo telehealth, cash pay.** 20 visits a week at an average of $350, worked 48 weeks a year. **Gross: $336,000.** At 15% overhead that nets roughly **$285,000**. Cash pay is the highest-margin model and the hardest one to fill.

> **Scenario 2: solo telehealth, insurance-based.** 32 visits a week at an average reimbursement of $150, worked 48 weeks. **Gross: $230,400.** At 30% overhead for billing, credentialing, and EHR, that nets roughly **$161,000**. A panel this size usually takes two to three years to build.

> **Scenario 3: part-time practice alongside a W2 job.** 12 cash-pay visits a week at $250, worked 46 weeks, on top of a three-day-a-week employed role. **Practice gross: $138,000**, plus whatever the employed role pays. This is the lowest-risk way to start.

> **Scenario 4: first year, insurance-based.** Months 1 to 3 go to credentialing with no billable volume, and the caseload ramps through months 4 to 12. A first year that lands well below a full-panel figure is normal, not a warning sign.

## Startup Costs: What It Takes to Open

The figures below are planning estimates for budgeting, not quoted prices. Confirm the current amount with each vendor and agency before you commit.

### Telehealth Practice (Lowest Cost)

| Item | Cost |
| :--- | :--- |
| NPI, CAQH, and DEA registration | NPI and CAQH are free; the DEA sets its own registration fee, so verify the current amount with the DEA before you apply |
| Malpractice insurance | $1,500-$3,000/year (quote-dependent) |
| EHR platform (SimplePractice, TherapyNotes) | $50-$150/month |
| Business entity formation (LLC/PLLC) | $100-$500 (your state's business filing office sets the fee) |
| HIPAA-compliant video platform | $0-$50/month (often included in EHR) |
| Website + basic marketing | $500-$2,000 |
| **Total first-year startup** | **$3,000-$8,000** |

### Office-Based Practice

| Item | Cost |
| :--- | :--- |
| Everything above | $3,000-$8,000 |
| Office lease (12 months) | $12,000-$36,000 |
| Furniture and equipment | $3,000-$10,000 |
| Front desk staff or virtual assistant | $25,000-$45,000/year |
| **Total first-year startup** | **$40,000-$100,000** |

**The math is clear:** Telehealth private practice has a dramatically lower barrier to entry than office-based. Many PMHNPs start with telehealth and add an office later (or never).

## W2 vs 1099: Tax Implications

The distinction between W2 (employed) and 1099 (independent contractor/practice owner) dramatically affects your take-home pay:

| Factor | W2 Employed | 1099 / Practice Owner |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Self-employment tax** | Employer pays half (7.65%) | You pay both halves (15.3%) |
| **Tax deductions** | Limited | Extensive (home office, equipment, travel, CME) |
| **Health insurance** | Employer-provided | Self-purchased (deductible) |
| **Retirement** | 401(k) with match | SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k), within the annual cap the IRS sets |
| **Effective tax saving** | Not applicable | An S-Corp election can cut self-employment tax |

**Pro tip:** Most private practice PMHNPs should look at an **S-Corp** election once the practice is consistently profitable. It changes how much of your profit is exposed to self-employment tax. How much that is worth depends entirely on your own profit and payroll, so price it with a healthcare CPA rather than a rule of thumb.

## Timeline: How Long Until Full Income?

| Phase | Timeline | Where the money is |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Credentialing + setup | Months 1-3 | No billable volume yet; budget for zero practice income |
| Building caseload | Months 3-9 | A thin fraction of a full panel |
| Half-panel | Months 9-15 | Roughly half your target volume |
| Full panel | Month 15+ | Full target volume |

**Reality check:** Most private practices take **12-18 months** to reach full earning potential. Smart PMHNPs keep their employed position (at least part-time) while building their practice.

## The Private Practice Income Spectrum

Private practice PMHNP income varies enormously based on practice model, payer mix, location, and hours worked. A part-time cash-pay telehealth practice run alongside an employed position is a supplement, not a salary. A full-time insurance-based solo practice, once the panel is full, becomes the main event. A group practice owner with three to five employed providers adds a margin on other people's billings on top of their own clinical revenue, which is where the largest numbers come from. The key variable is always the same: the ratio of clinical time spent seeing patients versus time spent on administrative and business operations. For measured figures, see [advertised pay by state](/salary-guide).

## Understanding Your Value: Collections Data

Before deciding on income expectations, work out what a single PMHNP provider generates in revenue:

Take a PMHNP seeing 30 patients per week at an average reimbursement of $200 per session, across 52 weeks. That is approximately **$312,000 in annual collections**. At overhead of $80,000 to $120,000, the net to the provider is $192,000 to $232,000. Change any input and the answer moves: this is arithmetic you can redo with your own numbers, not a benchmark.

For employed PMHNPs, a "collections split" of roughly 45% to 55% to the provider is a common structure in private practice, though it is negotiated rather than standardised. On the example above, a 50% split on $312,000 in collections is $156,000. Knowing your revenue generation power gives you enormous leverage in salary negotiations: you can demonstrate exactly how much revenue you produce versus how much you are paid.

### Key insight for contract negotiation
If an employer offers you $145,000 and you will be generating $300,000 in collections, you are receiving less than half of what you produce. That gap is standard for employed positions with full benefits, but it means you have room to negotiate upward, especially once you can point to your own collections data after 6-12 months.

## Tax Strategy for Private Practice PMHNPs

Understanding tax optimization is critical because private practice income is subject to self-employment tax (15.3%, set by federal statute) on top of federal and state income taxes. Here are the most impactful strategies:

### S-Corporation Election
Once your practice is consistently profitable, converting from a sole proprietorship or single-member LLC to an S-Corp can reduce the self-employment tax you owe. The strategy involves paying yourself a "reasonable salary" (subject to payroll tax) and taking the remaining profit as a shareholder distribution (not subject to SE tax). How much it saves depends entirely on your profit and the salary you can defend, so consult a healthcare CPA for the optimal salary-to-distribution ratio.

### Key Deductions
Private practice PMHNPs can deduct all ordinary and necessary business expenses:
- **Home office deduction:** if you have a dedicated office space, deduct a proportionate share of rent, mortgage interest, utilities, and insurance.
- **Professional development:** CME courses, conferences, board certification fees, journal subscriptions, and clinical supervision costs.
- **Technology:** EHR subscriptions, telehealth platform fees, computer equipment, and clinical reference tools (UpToDate, Epocrates).
- **Professional services:** billing company fees, CPA fees, legal counsel, and malpractice insurance premiums.
- **Vehicle:** mileage for clinical supervision meetings, networking events, and any off-site clinical work.

### Retirement Planning
Private practice owners can contribute significantly more to retirement than employed PMHNPs:
- **Solo 401(k):** combined employer and employee contributions, up to the annual limit the IRS sets. Check the current figure with the IRS before planning around it.
- **SEP IRA:** up to 25% of net self-employment income, within the IRS annual cap
- **Defined benefit plan:** the most powerful tax deferral tool available to high earners, with contribution limits set annually by the IRS

A PMHNP running a high-profit S-Corp can defer a substantial share of income through a combination of solo 401(k) employer contributions and a defined benefit plan. How much you can defer, and what it saves, depends on your age, profit, and payroll, so model it with a CPA rather than a rule of thumb.

## Scaling Beyond Solo Practice

Once your solo practice is stable and profitable, there are several paths to scale income beyond what a single provider can generate:

### 1. Hire Additional Providers
Employing or contracting other PMHNPs or therapists is the most common scaling strategy. A common model pays employed PMHNPs roughly 45% to 55% of collections, with the practice retaining the rest. Whether an added provider is profitable depends on how fast they fill a panel, what your overhead is, and how long you carry them while they ramp.

### 2. Group Practice Model
Transition from solo to group practice by adding 2-4 providers, a practice manager, and shared overhead. Group practices benefit from economies of scale: shared EHR, billing infrastructure, office space, and marketing costs are distributed across providers, so per-provider overhead falls as you add clinicians.

### 3. Telehealth Expansion
Add providers in other states to expand your geographic reach without physical office expansion. A PMHNP in Texas and another in California can operate under the same practice umbrella, sharing billing and administrative services while serving different patient populations.

### 4. Passive Income Streams
Diversify beyond clinical revenue with:
- **Supervision fees:** charging new NPs or interns a monthly fee for clinical supervision, where your state allows it
- **Speaking and consulting:** grand rounds, CME presentations, or expert witness testimony, usually billed by the hour
- **Online courses:** developing self-paced training modules on topics like MAT prescribing, documentation best practices, or starting a private practice
- **Precepting fees:** some programs pay a stipend per student per semester for clinical preceptors, at a rate the school sets

### How Income Builds by Practice Model

| Model | Year 1 | Year 3 | Year 5+ |
|-------|-----------|-----------|------------|
| Solo (employed) | Your employer's band from day one | Band plus raises | Band plus raises |
| Solo private practice | Below employed pay while you ramp | Ahead of employed pay with a full panel | Set by your panel and your margin |
| Group practice owner (2-3 providers) | Your clinical income, minus setup | Clinical income plus a margin on each provider | Margin scales with headcount |
| Multi-site/franchise model | Heavy reinvestment, thin take-home | Management income overtakes clinical | Income is mostly business, not clinical |

The highest-earning PMHNPs in the country are rarely the best clinicians. They are good clinicians who also learned business. Private practice is not for everyone, but for those willing to invest in entrepreneurial skills alongside clinical excellence, the financial ceiling is significantly higher than any employed position.
